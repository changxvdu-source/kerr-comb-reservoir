\section{Driving both directions of the ring}\label{sec:bidir}

Section~\ref{sec:sweetspot} shows that the signal and the detuning jitter act on the single-direction ring through the same
variable, the effective detuning. A ring has a second set of modes that shares this detuning: the counter-propagating ones. Pumped
from the same laser, both directions see the same detuning jitter and source intensity noise, while the signal can be put on their
difference. This section tests whether that helps.

\subsection{Model}

Each direction carries its own intracavity field. Counter-propagating fields walk through each other once per round trip, so each
sees the other only through its round-trip-averaged power (cross-phase modulation, factor 2):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}E_X}{\mathrm{d}n} = \hat{L}E_X + i\gamma L\left(|E_X|^2 + 2\langle|E_Y|^2\rangle\right)E_X + \sqrt{\theta_1}\,E_{\mathrm{in},X},
\qquad (X,Y)\in\{(\mathrm{cw},\mathrm{ccw}),(\mathrm{ccw},\mathrm{cw})\},
\label{eq:bidir}
\end{equation}
where $E_X(\tau)$ is the fast-time field of direction $X$, $\hat L$ acts on its Fourier components as the linear terms (loss, detuning,
dispersion) of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lle}, and $\langle|E_Y|^2\rangle=t_R^{-1}\int_0^{t_R}|E_Y|^2\,\mathrm{d}\tau$ is the round-trip-averaged power
of the other direction. This coupling underlies directional bistability~\cite{kaplan1982}, the spontaneous symmetry breaking of
counter-propagating light in Kerr resonators~\cite{delbino2017} and counter-propagating solitons~\cite{yang2017}. We neglect backscattering. The detuning
jitter and the source intensity noise are common to both directions; the quantum field noise and the detection noise are
independent. Each direction leaves through its own drop port (Fig.~\ref{fig:bidir}a), so $2\times31$ lines are detected ($62\times8$ readout features). In the
schemes that use both directions the power of the operating point is split equally between them. With the counter-propagating field
switched off the simulation reproduces the single-direction simulator exactly (identical outputs without noise).

We compare, on the same random operating points of the mask group with stronger drop coupling:
\emph{one direction} (31 or 62 lines read; the latter matches the feature count of the two-direction schemes);
\emph{two directions, inverted signal} (the counter-propagating direction carries the same mask with $u\to1-u$);
\emph{two directions, signal on one side} (the counter-propagating direction carries the mask with a constant symbol);
\emph{two directions, independent masks}; and, as a control for the coupling, \emph{two separate rings, inverted signal} (the drives
of the inverted scheme on two uncoupled rings fed from one laser: same features, same common noise, no cross-phase modulation).

\subsection{Random search}

Table~\ref{tab:bidir}(a) uses 768 random operating points per scheme (the same points for all), with the protocol of
Table~\ref{tab:encodings}: choice among the best eight of the first run pair on three new pairs, report on three new pairs on each of
three series, with and without the detuning jitter. Inverting the signal in the second channel is what matters: two separate rings
driven this way are four times better than one ring read out with the same number of lines, and the two directions of one ring are a
further 1.7 times better. Putting the signal on one side only gives no gain ($2.0\times10^{-4}$ on a smaller search of 192 points,
where one direction reaches $1.6\times10^{-4}$), and independent masks in the two directions give little ($1.4\times10^{-4}$). Good
two-direction points are rare: among the 768 points, 8 reach a single-pair validation error below $10^{-4}$ (two separate rings: 4;
one direction: none), and two smaller searches of 192 points each found $2.4\times10^{-5}$ and $1.8\times10^{-4}$ for the
two-direction scheme, against $8.0\times10^{-5}$ and $7.7\times10^{-5}$ for two separate rings.

The gain does not come from rejecting the common jitter: the jitter penalty (error with jitter over error without) is 2 to 3 for
every scheme. It comes from better computation, i.e.\ lower error without jitter. A plausible mechanism is the cross-phase term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bidir}: it shifts the detuning of every line of one direction by the round-trip-averaged power of the other, which with an
inverted signal responds to the input with the opposite sign. The signal therefore acts on the effective detuning through two paths of
opposite sign, a global, input-dependent gating term that adds products between the two directions' features. We have not separated
this further.

\subsection{Robustness and the role of the mask}

Around the best two-direction point of the first search (Table~\ref{tab:bidir}(a) uses a different point from the larger search) we
varied one parameter at a time, each on two pairs of new runs on three series ($2.5\times10^{-5}$ at the point itself). The error stays
between $1.7\times10^{-5}$ and $3.8\times10^{-5}$ for a normalised detuning from 1.5 to 2.1 (it rises steeply beyond 2.2, to about $10^{-3}$), for 0.8 to 1.1 times the power, for a
modulation depth of at least 0.9 times the original, for a side-line power fraction within $\pm0.2$, and for an input coupling
$\log_{10}\Qone$ from $-0.1$ to $+0.2$ around its value. The operating point is therefore a broad region, not a narrow
peak. The mask is not interchangeable: with the same physical parameters, 16 other random masks give $9.8\times10^{-5}$ to
$8.7\times10^{-3}$. This suggests that good points are rare mainly because good masks are rare.

Since the mask is written by a programmable spectral shaper, it can be chosen like a trained parameter. Table~\ref{tab:bidir}(b) gives
each scheme the same optimisation budget at its best operating point (detuning moved to 1.73 for the two-direction scheme, inside the
plateau above). Optimisation helps every scheme, by 2.3 to 5.7 times on the held-out series, and narrows the gaps: two separate rings
are 2.9 times better than one direction, and the two directions of one ring a further 1.55 times, on the threshold that we treat as a
tie. The best result, $7.3\times10^{-6}$ with jitter, is 21 times below the best single ring of Section~\ref{sec:readout}
($1.55\times10^{-4}$). This factor combines several changes and should not be read as the effect of the second direction alone: the
readout doubles (62 instead of 31 lines), the mask is optimised, the second direction is driven, and the evaluation uses held-out series
with fewer run pairs. Like for like, the two directions are 4.5 times better than one direction with the same readout size and the
same mask optimisation, and 1.55 times better than two separate rings.

\subsection{Input delay lines: moving time to frequency}

Feeding the input lines with the signal delayed by different amounts puts several past symbols into the resonator at once, so that
four-wave mixing forms products across time (Section~\ref{sec:kernel}). We delayed the lines $|\mu|=3$--4, 5--6 and 7--8 by one,
two and three steps of $s$ symbols ($|\mu|=1$--2 undelayed) and repeated the random search (192 points, same points for all). For one
direction (62 lines) the error falls from $2.4\times10^{-4}$ ($s=0$) to $1.0\times10^{-4}$ ($s=1$) and $6.1\times10^{-5}$ ($s=2$);
for two directions with inverted signal only from $2.1\times10^{-5}$ to $1.6\times10^{-5}$ and $1.7\times10^{-5}$. Delay lines thus
supply much of what the single direction lacks, while the two-direction scheme gains little from them.

\subsection{Against equally optimised software}\label{sec:bidir-software}

A fair comparison has to give the software the same optimisation. We optimised the input weights and biases of a 62-node ESN (its
counterpart of the mask; the recurrent matrix stays fixed, as the resonator's physics does) with the same procedure and budget, at
four levels of input noise, and report on the same held-out series. The optimised ESN reaches $1.7\times10^{-7}$, $6.6\times10^{-7}$,
$6.5\times10^{-6}$ and $4.5\times10^{-5}$ at input noise $10^{-4}$, $3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$ and $3\times10^{-3}$ (random input
weights: $2.5\times10^{-6}$, $5.5\times10^{-6}$, $2.2\times10^{-5}$ and $8.7\times10^{-5}$). The best two-direction ring
($7.3\times10^{-6}$) therefore matches the equally optimised ESN of the same readout dimension at an input noise of about $10^{-3}$,
i.e.\ about 8 effective bits by the conversion of Section~\ref{sec:software}, one bit more than the best single ring with a random
mask. At 9 to 10 bits, the resolution of converters at these rates, the ESN is 3 to 11 times better. Two directions, mask
optimisation and delay lines narrow the gap to software of the same size, but do not close it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bidir}b).
